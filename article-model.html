How ImmunoBlitz Models the Immune System

What the game represents, what it simplifies and why those choices matter for learning.

Published by BrainStorm Games · Last reviewed 8 October 2026

Every educational game is a model. It selects some parts of reality, simplifies them, and turns them into rules that a player can understand and interact with. ImmunoBlitz does this with the human immune system, infection and public health.

The real immune system contains enormous numbers of cells, signalling molecules, tissues and feedback loops. Pathogens reproduce on different timescales, immune responses vary between people, and treatments do not behave like instant game actions. A faithful simulation of every detail would be scientifically impressive—but it would not necessarily be a useful learning game.

Key idea

A model is useful because it simplifies. The important question is not whether a model includes everything, but whether its simplifications help learners understand the relationships it was designed to represent.

1. What Does ImmunoBlitz Try to Represent?

At its core, ImmunoBlitz represents a conflict between infectious threats and the body's protective systems. Players see pathogens as identifiable threats, body health as something that can be monitored, and defensive or medical interventions as strategic choices.

These visible elements make invisible biological processes easier to discuss. A learner cannot watch a macrophage working inside their own tissues, but a game can create a simplified situation in which the consequences of weak or strong defence become apparent.

2. Why Use Waves?

Real infections do not arrive in neat numbered waves. The wave structure is a game-design choice that creates pacing, increasing difficulty and moments for decision-making.

Educationally, waves can help learners notice that the body is dealing with changing pressure over time. The scientific concept is not “infection happens in rounds”; it is that pathogen burden and immune pressure can change as an infection develops.

3. Why Turn Health Into Numbers?

Human organs do not have visible health bars. Health bars translate a complicated biological state into a signal the player can read quickly. They show that damage can accumulate and that failing to control infection has consequences for the wider body.

The limitation is that real organ function is not a single percentage. Different tissues respond differently to infection, and clinical health is measured using many observations and tests.

4. Why Turn Defences Into Upgrades?

The body does not spend points to increase mucus, strengthen skin or activate immune cells. Upgrades are a strategy-game mechanic that gives the player agency and forces prioritisation.

The educational idea underneath the mechanic is that defence has layers and that strengthening one part of a system can influence how much pressure reaches another part. The upgrade itself is fictional; the relationship between layers of defence is real.

5. Why Include Different Pathogens?

Representing viruses, bacteria and fungi as different threat categories reinforces the idea that infectious agents are biologically diverse. They differ in structure, reproduction and treatment.

A game must exaggerate some differences to make them readable during play. The Learning Hub provides the more careful explanation so players do not confuse colour, speed or other visual mechanics with literal microbiology.

6. Why Include Medical and Public-Health Interventions?

Vaccination, antibiotics, hygiene and respiratory protection represent different points at which humans can influence infection. Including them helps shift the message away from “the immune system fights alone” toward a broader view that medicine and public health support disease prevention and control.

These interventions are intentionally simplified. A real antibiotic choice depends on the bacterium and resistance pattern. Vaccination takes time to generate immunity. Masks vary in fit and filtration. Antiseptics are used in specific ways. The game reduces these complexities so their broad purposes can be compared.

7. What the Model Leaves Out

No educational model is complete. ImmunoBlitz does not attempt to represent every cytokine, receptor, tissue, microbial species, genetic factor or clinical variable. It also does not reproduce the true timescales of infection or treatment.

Leaving things out is not automatically a flaw. Problems arise when learners mistake the simplified representation for a complete description of reality. That is why the site repeatedly distinguishes game mechanics from real biology.

8. A Useful Way to Critique the Game

Model-checking activity

Choose one mechanic in ImmunoBlitz and answer four questions:

  • What real biological idea is this mechanic trying to represent?
  • What has been simplified or exaggerated?
  • What might a player incorrectly assume if nobody explained the simplification?
  • How could the mechanic be improved without making the game too complicated?

9. Why This Matters in Science Education

Scientists use models constantly: diagrams of cells, climate models, population models and molecular structures all leave out details. Learning to ask what a model represents, what assumptions it makes and where it breaks down is an important scientific skill.

ImmunoBlitz can therefore teach two things at once: content about immunity and a broader lesson about how scientific models work.

10. The Best Way to Use ImmunoBlitz

Play, question, then investigate

The strongest learning cycle is not simply “play the game”. It is: play, notice a mechanic, ask what it represents, then use reliable scientific information to compare the model with reality.

This is also the purpose of the Learning Hub. The game creates the question; the articles provide the deeper biology.

Quick Check

  • Is ImmunoBlitz a literal simulation of the immune system? No.
  • Why are waves useful? They create pacing and make changing pressure visible.
  • Why use upgrades? They create player choice and represent layered defence in a simplified way.
  • What is an important scientific skill when using any model? Identifying what the model represents and where its simplifications stop matching reality.
