Research & Evidence Behind ImmunoBlitz

How educational research with middle-school science students informed the design and development of ImmunoBlitz.

Published by BrainStorm Games

ImmunoBlitz was developed not only as an educational game, but also as a research-informed attempt to explore how digital games can support engagement with middle-school science. The project grew from a simple question: can a science videogame make difficult, largely invisible biological processes more engaging and easier for students to discuss?

The game was evaluated through a small-scale educational research study focusing on students' motivation, flow and enjoyment while learning about microorganisms and the human immune system. Rather than treating the game as a finished solution, the research was used to examine how students actually responded to game-based learning and where the design could be improved.

Research principle

The purpose of the study was not to prove that one videogame can automatically improve science learning. It was to investigate how students experienced the game, what they found engaging, what limited their motivation, and how those experiences could inform better educational game design.

1. What Was the Research About?

The study explored students' experiences of learning science through both conventional classroom activities and game-informed learning. Particular attention was given to three closely related areas: motivation, flow and enjoyment.

These ideas matter because students can understand a topic without necessarily enjoying the learning process, and they can enjoy an activity without necessarily remaining deeply focused on it. Studying these experiences separately helped create a more detailed picture of how learners responded to ImmunoBlitz.

  • Motivation: what encouraged students to begin, continue or avoid an activity.
  • Flow: the experience of becoming deeply focused and absorbed in an activity.
  • Enjoyment: how positively students experienced the learning activity itself.

2. Who Took Part?

The study involved 24 middle-school science students aged approximately 11 to 12. The students attended the same Maltese state middle school and were organised into six small focus groups of four students.

The research included both control and experimental participants. This made it possible to compare students who experienced the immune-system topic with the additional ImmunoBlitz activity against students who learned the topic without that extra gameplay experience.

Why this matters

The sample was deliberately small and came from one school. The findings therefore provide useful insight into these students' experiences, but they should not be treated as evidence that every learner would respond in exactly the same way.

3. What Did the Experimental Group Do?

Students in the experimental condition were given access to ImmunoBlitz while studying the immune-system topic and were asked to play the game at home over approximately two weeks.

The control group continued learning the same broad science topic without this additional gameplay experience. Students were then invited to discuss their experiences, including which learning activities they preferred, what they found motivating, and how they felt about educational videogames.

The research therefore looked beyond whether students simply liked or disliked the game. It examined how gameplay fitted into their wider experience of school science and how the setting in which the game was used influenced their response.

4. How Was the Research Analysed?

Students took part in small-group discussions in which they could describe their experiences in their own words. The discussions were recorded and transcribed, and students were able to communicate naturally using Maltese and English.

The resulting data were analysed thematically. Instead of reducing every response to a single score, the analysis looked for recurring patterns across the discussions.

Particular attention was given to themes related to:

  • motivation and willingness to participate;
  • enjoyment of the science topic;
  • engagement and concentration;
  • preferred learning activities;
  • perceptions of educational videogames;
  • the difference between learning at school and playing at home.

Why use student discussions?

A student's experience cannot always be understood from a test score alone. Small-group discussions allowed students to explain why they enjoyed or disliked an activity, what distracted them, what encouraged them to continue and how they compared different ways of learning.

5. What Did the Study Find?

The findings were more nuanced than simply saying that games make learning better. Students frequently valued learning activities that were interactive, practical, collaborative and visually engaging. Educational games were generally viewed positively when students felt that the game itself was enjoyable and meaningfully connected to what they were learning.

Students in the experimental group reported greater enjoyment of the immune-system topic than the comparison group. However, the research could not establish that ImmunoBlitz alone caused that difference. A small exploratory study cannot isolate every influence on how students experience a topic.

One particularly useful finding was that simply giving students an educational game to play at home did not automatically produce strong motivation. Students had competing demands on their free time, other forms of entertainment available to them, and different levels of interest in voluntarily returning to an educational activity outside school.

Students often expressed stronger enthusiasm for educational games when imagining them being used as part of the school experience, where gameplay could be combined with classmates, discussion and teacher support.

6. What Did We Learn From the Research?

Educational games still need to be good games

Students did not automatically value a game because it contained educational content. Gameplay, challenge, presentation, feedback and player choice still mattered. An educational objective cannot compensate for an experience that the learner does not want to continue playing.

Context matters

The same game can produce a different experience depending on where and how it is used. A game used during a lesson can generate discussion, competition, collaboration and teacher questioning. At home, it competes with homework, hobbies, social activities and commercial entertainment.

Interaction can support engagement

Students repeatedly valued active forms of learning, including games, experiments, teamwork and other activities in which they were doing something rather than only receiving information.

A game should complement teaching

The findings supported using ImmunoBlitz as part of a wider learning experience rather than positioning the game as a replacement for classroom teaching. Gameplay can introduce questions and create memorable situations, while discussion and reliable scientific resources can provide the depth and clarification that a simplified game cannot.

7. Limitations of the Study

The research had several important limitations, and these need to be considered when interpreting the findings.

  • Small sample: only 24 students participated.
  • Single setting: participants came from one school, limiting how widely the findings can be generalised.
  • Researcher involvement: the researcher was also involved in teaching the students and developing ImmunoBlitz, creating a possible source of researcher bias.
  • Home use: the experimental activity relied substantially on students choosing to play outside school, where free time and competing activities affected participation.
  • Focus of the study: the research concentrated primarily on motivation, flow and enjoyment rather than demonstrating a measurable increase in immune-system knowledge.

Interpreting the evidence carefully

The study should therefore be understood as an exploratory educational evaluation. It provided valuable evidence about learners' experiences and informed the development of ImmunoBlitz, but it does not establish that playing the game will automatically improve motivation or academic achievement for every student.

8. How the Findings Influenced ImmunoBlitz

Research is most useful when it leads to better design. The study reinforced several principles that continue to shape ImmunoBlitz and the resources surrounding it.

Research insightHow it influences ImmunoBlitz
Educational value alone does not guarantee motivation.Gameplay, progression, player decisions and feedback need to remain important parts of the design.
Students value active and interactive learning.The game asks players to make decisions and respond to changing threats rather than simply read information.
Context influences engagement.ImmunoBlitz is presented as a resource that can be integrated into classroom discussion rather than only assigned for unsupervised home use.

9. From Research Project to Educational Resource

The current ImmunoBlitz project brings together three connected stages of learning.

Play → Question → Investigate

Play: students interact with a simplified immune-system model through the game.

Question: learners identify what the game represents accurately and where gameplay has simplified reality.

Investigate: the Learning Hub and classroom activities provide deeper scientific explanations and opportunities for discussion.

This approach treats the videogame as the beginning of a learning conversation rather than the end of one. A player might first encounter a concept through an upgrade, pathogen or intervention in the game, then question how accurately it represents biology, and finally investigate the real science in more detail.

10. Why Research Transparency Matters

Educational technology is sometimes promoted using broad claims about engagement, motivation or learning. The research behind ImmunoBlitz does not support the claim that simply playing one educational videogame will automatically improve these outcomes.

Instead, the study suggests that students' experiences depend on several interacting factors: the quality of the game, the learner's own interests, how the activity is introduced, where it is used and how it connects with the rest of the learning experience.

Being transparent about both positive findings and limitations is important. ImmunoBlitz is therefore presented as a research-informed educational game, not as a proven substitute for teaching or as a guaranteed method for increasing achievement.

11. Continuing Development

The research provided a starting point rather than an endpoint. ImmunoBlitz continues to develop as the game, website and accompanying resources are refined.

Future improvements can build on the same principle used in the original study: observe how learners actually interact with the experience, identify where design assumptions do not match real behaviour, and use that evidence to improve both gameplay and educational value.
