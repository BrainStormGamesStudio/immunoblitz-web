Inflammation: Why We Get Red, Hot, Swollen and Sore

The visible signs of a hidden immune response.

Published by BrainStorm Games · Last reviewed 8 October 2026

Redness, heat, swelling and pain are often treated as signs that something has gone wrong. In one sense they are: they can appear when tissue is injured or infected. But these changes are also evidence that the body's defence and repair systems are active. This coordinated response is called inflammation.

Inflammation is a major part of innate immunity. It can begin rapidly after cells detect injury, infection or other danger. Its purpose is to bring defensive molecules and cells to the affected tissue, help contain the threat and start repair.

Key idea

Inflammation is not the same thing as infection. Infection can trigger inflammation, but inflammation can also occur after non-infectious injury such as a sprain, burn or tissue damage.

1. The Classic Signs of Inflammation

The traditional signs are redness, heat, swelling and pain; loss of function is sometimes included as a fifth sign. These visible effects arise from changes in local blood vessels and signalling molecules.

SignWhat contributes to it?
RednessBlood vessels widen, increasing local blood flow.
HeatIncreased blood flow raises the temperature of the affected area.
SwellingBlood vessels become more permeable, allowing fluid and proteins to enter tissues.
PainInflammatory mediators and tissue pressure stimulate pain-sensitive nerves.
Loss of functionPain, swelling and tissue damage can reduce normal movement or activity.

2. How Does the Body Know Something Is Wrong?

Cells of the innate immune system carry receptors that recognise common molecular patterns associated with pathogens or damaged cells. Tissue-resident cells such as macrophages can respond by releasing signalling molecules including cytokines and other inflammatory mediators.

These signals change nearby blood vessels and help recruit additional immune cells from the bloodstream. The response is therefore both local and coordinated.

3. Why Do Blood Vessels Become Leaky?

Greater vascular permeability allows plasma proteins and defensive molecules to move into the affected tissue. At the same time, the lining of blood vessels changes in ways that make it easier for circulating white blood cells to attach and move out of the bloodstream.

This is useful because immune cells need physical access to the site of infection or injury. The downside is swelling: fluid enters the tissue along with the molecules and cells needed for defence.

4. Neutrophils and Macrophages Arrive

Neutrophils are often among the earliest cells recruited from the blood during acute inflammation. They can engulf microorganisms and release antimicrobial substances. Macrophages can also engulf material, clear dead cells and release signals that shape the response.

This links inflammation directly to the cellular defenders described elsewhere in the Learning Hub. Redness and swelling are not separate from immune action; they are part of the process that gets immune cells and molecules where they are needed.

5. Is More Inflammation Always Better?

No. A controlled inflammatory response can be protective, but excessive or prolonged inflammation can damage healthy tissue. Acute inflammation normally helps remove the cause of injury and begin repair. If the trigger persists or regulation fails, inflammation can become chronic.

Many diseases involve inappropriate or long-lasting inflammation, which is why the immune response needs both activation and control.

6. Fever: A Whole-Body Response

Inflammatory signalling can also affect the whole body. One possible result is fever, an increase in the body's regulated temperature. Fever can occur during infection as part of a systemic immune response.

Fever is not simply the body “overheating” because germs are present. It involves coordinated physiological changes. Its significance depends on the person, the temperature and the clinical context.

7. Inflammation and Healing

Defence and repair overlap. Once a threat is controlled, immune cells help clear damaged material and signals shift toward tissue repair. Fibroblasts, blood vessels and extracellular matrix components contribute to rebuilding the affected area.

This means the inflammatory response has a beginning, a working phase and ideally a resolution phase. Successful immunity is not only about starting a strong response; it is also about ending it appropriately.

8. How This Connects to ImmunoBlitz

Damage can trigger defence

ImmunoBlitz represents the body as a system under pressure from infection. In real biology, tissue damage and pathogen detection cause chemical communication that recruits immune defences. Inflammation is one of the major processes linking detection to action.

A game may represent damage using health bars or other visual signals, but actual inflammation is driven by cells, blood vessels and signalling molecules. The important connection is that the body does not passively wait for pathogens to disappear; it changes the local environment to bring resources to the problem.

Inflammation is useful—but complicated

Do not interpret redness or swelling as proof of a particular infection. Many different conditions can cause inflammation. ImmunoBlitz is a science-learning model, not a diagnostic tool.

9. Quick Check

  • Is inflammation always caused by infection? No. Injury and other non-infectious triggers can also cause it.
  • Why does an inflamed area become red and warm? Local blood vessels widen and blood flow increases.
  • Why does swelling occur? Increased vascular permeability allows fluid and proteins into tissues.
  • Which cells often arrive early? Neutrophils are major early responders in acute inflammation.
  • Why must inflammation resolve? Prolonged or excessive inflammation can damage healthy tissue.
