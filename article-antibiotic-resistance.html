Antibiotics and Antimicrobial Resistance

Why bacteria can evolve around medicines—and why the patient is not the thing that becomes resistant.

Published by BrainStorm Games · Last reviewed 8 October 2026

Antibiotics transformed medicine by making many bacterial infections treatable. But bacteria can evolve. When bacterial populations develop the ability to survive medicines that once killed or inhibited them, treatment becomes more difficult. This is known as antibiotic resistance, one part of the broader problem of antimicrobial resistance.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) can involve bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites becoming less responsive to medicines designed to treat them. Antibiotic resistance refers specifically to resistance involving bacteria and antibacterial drugs.

Key idea

The person does not become resistant to an antibiotic. The microorganisms change. Resistant microbes survive and reproduce, allowing resistance traits to become more common in the population.

1. How Does Resistance Develop?

Within a bacterial population there may be genetic differences. Mutations can arise, and bacteria can also exchange genetic material. Some of these changes may allow a bacterium to survive exposure to a particular antibiotic.

When the antibiotic is used, susceptible bacteria are more likely to be killed or inhibited. Resistant bacteria have a survival advantage and can reproduce. This process is an example of natural selection.

2. Does Antibiotic Use Cause Every Mutation?

No. Resistance mutations and resistance genes can exist before a particular antibiotic is used. Antibiotic exposure acts as a selection pressure: it favours bacteria that already have or acquire traits allowing them to survive.

This distinction matters because it connects antimicrobial resistance directly to evolution. The medicine does not “teach” an individual bacterium how to resist it in the way a person learns a skill. Instead, bacterial populations change over generations.

3. How Can Bacteria Resist Antibiotics?

Different resistance mechanisms affect different drugs. Bacteria may produce enzymes that destroy or modify an antibiotic, change the molecule the drug normally targets, reduce the amount of drug entering the cell, or actively pump the drug back out.

Because antibiotics work in different ways, resistance is not one single mechanism. A bacterium can be resistant to one drug while remaining susceptible to another, and some bacteria accumulate resistance to multiple antibiotics.

4. Why Overuse and Misuse Matter

Every unnecessary exposure creates additional opportunities for selection. Using antibiotics when they are not needed—for example, for a viral infection—provides no benefit against the virus while still exposing bacteria in or around the body to the drug.

Inappropriate antibiotic use can therefore speed the spread of resistance. Responsible use means using the right medicine only when it is needed and following appropriate professional guidance.

5. Resistance Can Spread Between Bacteria

Bacteria reproduce rapidly, but resistance can also spread through horizontal gene transfer. Genetic material carrying resistance genes can sometimes move between bacterial cells, including between related species.

This means resistance is not only about one bacterial lineage slowly accumulating mutations. Mobile pieces of DNA can help useful resistance traits move through microbial populations.

6. Why AMR Is a Public-Health Problem

When first-choice treatments stop working, infections may last longer, require different medicines or become more difficult to control. Resistant infections can increase the risk of complications and can spread between people.

Modern healthcare also depends on reliable antimicrobial treatment. Surgery, cancer therapy, transplantation and intensive care are safer when bacterial infections can be prevented and treated effectively.

7. What Can Reduce the Pressure for Resistance?

  • Use antibiotics only when they are appropriate for a bacterial infection.
  • Follow the prescribed dose and duration when an antibiotic is medically indicated.
  • Prevent infections through hygiene, vaccination, food safety and infection-control measures.
  • Use diagnostics where appropriate to help identify the cause of infection.
  • Monitor resistance patterns so healthcare systems can adapt treatment guidance.

AMR is also a One Health issue because antimicrobial use and resistant organisms can involve humans, animals, agriculture and the environment.

8. How This Connects to ImmunoBlitz

Match the intervention to the threat

ImmunoBlitz can reinforce an important distinction: antibiotics are aimed at bacteria, not viruses. That simple decision point can lead to a much larger classroom discussion about responsible antibiotic use and evolution.

A strategy game simplifies treatment into clear choices. Real clinical decisions are more complicated: doctors may need symptoms, examination findings, laboratory tests, local resistance data and patient history before deciding whether an antibiotic is appropriate.

Resistance is evolutionary, not a “power-up”

In real populations, resistance emerges and spreads through genetic variation, selection and gene transfer over time. The educational goal is to understand the process, not to suggest that bacteria consciously adapt because they “want” to survive.

9. Quick Check

  • Who becomes resistant? The microorganisms, not the patient.
  • What provides the selection pressure? Exposure to an antimicrobial drug.
  • Why do antibiotics not treat viruses? Viruses do not have the bacterial structures and processes targeted by antibiotics.
  • Can resistance genes move between bacteria? Yes, some can spread through horizontal gene transfer.
  • Why is AMR more than an individual problem? Resistant organisms can spread and reduce treatment options across communities and healthcare systems.
